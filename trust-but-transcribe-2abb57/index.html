Trust, but transcribe: what I learned when my app started speaking Italian

My wife wanted to learn Italian, so I built her a small audio course app: an English instructor voice teaches, and a native Italian voice lands the examples. It worked great right up until it didn't, and the ways it failed taught me more about building non-English features than anything I've read. Short of it is: if your app speaks a language you don't, you need to give your pipeline ears. Here's how I got there.

The bug that started it

Single Italian words kept getting clipped. A lesson would say "the word for to eat is..." and then nothing, or a chopped fragment where "mangiare" should be. Full Italian sentences were fine. Just the lone words died.

Turns out the text-to-speech API (Deepgram's Aura-2 Italian voices, as of late July 2026) was returning silent audio for one-word requests about a third of the time, with a clean HTTP 200 like nothing was wrong. Two-word inputs never failed. That's the first lesson: an audio API can fail silently in the most literal sense, and your logs will show green across the board.

I graded the envelope instead of the letter

My first fix made things worse, and the way it went wrong is the part worth sharing. The plan was reasonable: request raw audio instead of mp3, measure the loudness of each render, and retry whenever a render came back silent.

The validation measured peak volume across the whole API response. But the response was a WAV file, and a WAV file starts with a text header (the letters RIFF and WAVE, sizes, format info). Read those header bytes as if they were audio samples and they look like a full-volume signal. Every render passed the loudness check, including the silent ones, because the check was listening to the file cabinet instead of the file. The "fixed" audio shipped with a click where the header got played as sound, followed by the same silence as before.

I tightened the validation to measure actual speech duration instead. That caught the silent renders and the truncated ones, and that's when the real problem surfaced.

The audio that passed every check was wrong

Renders that were loud enough and long enough were often mispronounced. I asked for "abitare" and got back something that transcribes as "avvitare" (to screw) or "auditare." When I transcribed a lesson that was already live, it had taught the word for "to understand" as "cacchiere," which is not an Italian word at all, and had swapped "prendo" (I take) for "credo" (I believe).

I don't speak Italian. My wife is just starting. Neither of us could have caught this by listening. The audio pipeline had every safeguard a programmer would think of (status codes, retries, duration checks, volume checks) and none of them can tell correct Italian from confident nonsense.

The fix for that blindness turned out to be cheap: transcribe your own audio and compare it to what you asked for. I ran every suspect render through two separate speech-to-text engines (a local Whisper model plus Deepgram's own nova-2, which meant their STT was convicting their TTS), on the theory that one model's blind spot shouldn't get to hide the other's. When both transcribers hear "avvitare," you asked for one word and got another. Trust, but transcribe.

Deterministic beats expressive when you're teaching

The deeper issue was the class of voice I was using. Generative TTS voices are expressive and natural, and they synthesize fresh audio every request, which means the same input can come out different (or wrong) on any given call. I tested all nine of Aura-2's Italian voices and couldn't find one that was reliably safe. Switching voices changes your odds, never your guarantee.

Older-style deterministic voices (I landed on Google's Neural2) return byte-identical audio for identical input, every time. They sound a little less human, and I'll take that trade without blinking, because a voice that cannot improvise cannot teach my wife a word that doesn't exist. If you're generating audio where correctness matters more than vibes (language learning, medical, legal, names), boring-reliable is the feature.

The same lesson, one layer up

The lesson text itself comes from an LLM, and the same worry applies: my generator model (GLM 5.2) writes English-flavored Italian sometimes ("chiedere una domanda," a word-for-word calque of "ask a question" that Italians don't say). So I added a second model as a reviewer that checks every generated script before it gets voiced.

Picking the reviewer was an afternoon and under a dollar of API calls. I planted known traps (false friends, calques, wrong-verb swaps) alongside clean control sentences, ran nine models over the set, then ran a harder second round to separate the models that aced the first. Gemini 3.6 Flash won it clean: every planted error caught, zero false alarms. Two other findings I'll carry forward: GLM missed exactly the error class it produces itself, so don't let a model grade its own homework. And Mistral Large confidently "corrected" a sentence that was already right (it insisted "sposato con Anna" should be "sposato a Anna," which is wrong), and that's worse than missing an error, because a reviewer that invents fixes injects the very bugs it exists to catch. A tiny homemade eval with planted answers beat every leaderboard I could have consulted, and it cost less than a coffee.

If you're building outside your language

  • Transcribe your own audio output and diff it against what you asked for. Two transcribers, different vendors.
  • Prefer deterministic voices when correctness outranks expressiveness.
  • Validate the content, not the container. Status codes, file sizes, and volume levels all lied to me in one week.
  • Review generated text with a different model than the one that wrote it, and test your reviewer with planted errors before you trust it.

The part that still gets me: every one of these checks is a few dozen lines an agent can write in minutes, and together they took a feature I couldn't verify with my own ears and made it trustworthy. I still can't speak Italian, and I can ship Italian I trust, because the pipeline has ears now. What's your app saying that you can't hear? Go find out.
