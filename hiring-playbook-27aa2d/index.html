00 · Overview

The through-line

One-page map. The rest is detail; this is the spine that connects it.

Hire the top performer for every seat, decide fast, and treat every candidate well on the way through. Five principles carry the whole thing:

  • Get crystal-clear on "success in this role" before I interview anyone. A scorecard — mission, ranked outcomes, competencies — is the yardstick every interview scores against. Most bad hires trace back to skipping this and interviewing on gut.
  • Run a progressive, weed-out-fast funnel. Each stage exists to decide one thing: do we invest in the next stage? Cheapest possible "no," earliest possible "no."
  • Hunt for evidence, not vibes. In the deep dive I'm chasing one question — was this person the driver of the results, or along for the ride? I ask "How?" until the story either holds or unravels.
  • Trust but verify. Reference every finalist by phone, pick some of the references myself, and listen for enthusiasm — faint praise is a bad reference.
  • Every candidate leaves respecting us — even the ones we never hire. Set the stage before and during every interview so no one is caught off guard. Today's "no" is tomorrow's referral.

And the discipline that makes it all work: when it's a maybe, it's a no. Protect the bar and my own time.

The funnel at a glance

Roughly 10–15 → 3–5 → 1–2 — screens yield deep dives yield finalists.

  • Stage 1 — Screen (15–30 min): rapport + basic fit. Worth another two hours?
  • Stage 2 — Résumé Deep Dive (60 min): job by job, oldest → newest. Were they the driver? Light technical depth.
  • Stage 3 — Team Fit + Project Presentation (60 min): meet the team, culture/DNA, present a real project.
  • Then: references → offer → onboarding.

My signature move: guardrail question banks

  • A bank of standard questions per job on the résumé.
  • Let the conversation wander naturally — one answer leads to the next question.
  • Steer to any question on the bank when the moment opens; don't march the list top-to-bottom.
  • Won't hit every question on every job — but across all jobs, cover each at least once.
  • The point: a real conversation where I dig when it matters, and still leave with enough to decide go / no-go.

10 · The books

What shaped how I hire

Ranked most → least influential. The thesis, the frameworks I use, and what I took from each.

1. Who — Geoff Smart & Randy Street

Thesis: Hiring is a skill you can systematize. Stop "voodoo hiring" on gut; gather verifiable performance data through a structured process — the A-Method — and you can reliably pick people with a ~90% chance of landing in the top 10%.

Frameworks I use

  • Scorecard — mission, 3–8 ranked outcomes, competencies. Replaces the job description; the yardstick for every interview.
  • A/B/C Players — my grading vocabulary. Everything scores back to the scorecard.
  • The Three P's — judge an accomplishment against prior year, the plan, and peers.
  • Push vs. Pull — did they leave pulled toward opportunity, or pushed out?
  • TORC (Threat of Reference Check) — "how will they rate you when we talk?" makes candidates tell the truth now.

What I took

Scorecard-first thinking; the relentless "was it really them, and how?" instinct; references as a forcing function during the interview, not just at the end.

"A Players tend to talk about outcomes linked to expectations. B and C Players talk generally about events, people they met, or aspects of the job they liked without ever getting into results."

2. Recruit Rockstars — Jeff Hyman

Thesis: Hire only Rockstars — the top ~5% you can afford — in every seat. A mediocre supporting cast drags down your best people. And recruiting is a sales problem as much as a vetting problem.

Frameworks I use

  • Career Deep Dive — the fixed per-role question set that is the backbone of my Stage 2.
  • 1–10 scoring on role fit + company fit; hard-cut below 7 (a 6 is really a 2).
  • Front-door vs. backdoor references — candidate-supplied vs. people I find myself. Backdoor is gold.
  • 3:10 rule — more than 3 companies in 10 years is a job-hopping flag (dig, don't auto-reject).
  • "When in doubt, throw them out" — the discipline behind maybe = no.

What I took

My whole Stage-2 progression; asking about references early; talking <20% of the time; treating recruiting as active selling, not passive filtering.

"Recruit five, get the results of ten, and pay them like eight."

3. Hiring for Attitude — Mark Murphy

Thesis: Skills can be trained; attitude usually can't. And most interview questions are so leading or clichéd they only measure how well someone recites a rehearsed answer.

Frameworks I use

  • "Could you tell me about a time you…" — and leave it hanging. Don't finish with "…and what did you do?"; that telegraphs the answer.
  • Brown Shorts questions — built from the moment high and low performers visibly diverge on our culture.
  • Problem bringers vs. problem solvers — do they just surface problems, or instinctively solve them?
  • Undifferentiating questions — if the answer can't separate a high performer from a low one, cut the question.

What I took

Kill leading phrasing; ban the canned-answer questions; design questions that differentiate for the role and culture I'm hiring for.

"When we explicitly or implicitly signal to our candidates what answer we're looking for, it stops them from revealing their true sentiments."

4. Hire Women — Debbie Madden

Thesis: De-biasing hiring is a concrete, tactical project — not a values statement — and it widens the pool of great people I can hire and keep.

Tactics I use

  • Trim the requirements list. Women apply at ~100% match, men at ~60%; every extra "nice-to-have" filters out qualified people.
  • One blind step — a name-blind work sample scored 1–5.
  • Don't anchor on salary history — use my own comp bands; never break internal pay equity.
  • Source from women-focused communities as an ongoing pipeline.
  • The "advocate for you" script at offer — a two-way accountability contract.

What I took

Audit the top of the funnel — posting language, requirement lists, sourcing — as seriously as the interviews. Make bias reduction a checklist item, not a good intention.

"Women will apply to a job if they believe they meet 100% of the job requirements, whereas men will apply if they believe they meet 60%."

20 · Strategy

The scorecard & the funnel

The what and why. How to run each interview is in section 30.

Start with a scorecard

No sourcing, no interviews, until this exists. It's the single yardstick everything scores against.

  • Mission — 1–5 sentences on why the role exists.
  • Outcomes — 3–8 specific, measurable, ranked results ("improve X from 7.1 to 9.0 by Dec 31"). Results, not tasks.
  • Competencies — the behaviors needed to hit those outcomes, plus a standing set of culture competencies on every scorecard.
  • Align & communicate — pressure-test against the business plan and adjacent roles; share it so everyone scores the same thing.

The progressive funnel

Each meeting has exactly one purpose — decide whether to have the next one. Roughly 10–15 → 3–5 → 1–2.

  • Stage 1 — Screen (15–30 min): rapport + basic fit. Worth another two hours?
  • Stage 2 — Deep Dive (60 min): job by job. Were they the driver? Did they understand X, not just do it?
  • Stage 3 — Team Fit + Project (60 min): culture/DNA, and they present a real project.

The funnel only works if each stage is allowed to end things. Don't advance someone because you feel bad, or because you've already invested time.

Two axes I'm scoring

  • Role fit — can they do the job? Competencies, technical depth, a track record of the scorecard outcomes.
  • Company / team fit — the DNA. Will they raise the bar, or drain the people around them?

A 1–10 on each is a useful forcing function: 9–10 Rockstar, 7–8 worth serious consideration, below 7 a cut.

Weed-out philosophy

  • When it's a maybe, it's a no. Don't burn an hour justifying a thumbs-down — "not the right fit," move on. The right candidate feels obvious.
  • Decide fast, in writing. Keep paste-ready reject reasons so review doesn't become paralysis.
  • Patterns over single data points: the 3:10 rule as a flag; push vs. pull on every exit; the Three P's on every accomplishment.
  • Batch your comparisons. Screen a set before deciding who advances, so it's apples to apples.

De-bias the top of the funnel

  • Trim requirements to true must-haves.
  • Run the posting through a gender-neutral language check.
  • Don't anchor on salary history; use my own comp bands.
  • Build in one blind step (name-blind work sample, scored 1–5).
  • Source from communities that widen the pool.

25 · Candidate experience

Set the stage

A pillar, not a nicety. It cuts across every stage.

Every candidate leaves respecting us — even the ones we never hire. The bar: they walk away thinking "this company really has their act together and treats candidates with respect." No one is ever caught off guard.

Why it matters

  • It's the right way to treat people, full stop.
  • Today's "no" is tomorrow's referral, re-applicant, or customer. Reputation compounds, and candidates talk.
  • A relaxed candidate shows their authentic self, not their nervous mask — better signal, and a fairer shot.
  • How we run the process is a live preview of how we run the company.

Before every stage — email ahead

  • What the conversation will look like: who they'll meet, how long, what we'll cover, what to prepare.
  • Stage 1: what the screen is for, how long, who's on the call.
  • Stage 2: we'll walk your roles oldest → newest — come ready to talk through each job.
  • Stage 3: you'll meet the team, and come ready to present a project (with the format and time set).

First few minutes — reiterate

  • Restate the agenda and timing.
  • Tell them I'll be taking notes so it doesn't read as disengagement.
  • Let them ask their burning questions early — it puts them at ease and reveals prep.

Throughout

  • Communicate promptly. Silence is how good companies create bad experiences.
  • Give a timely, respectful yes/no.
  • Reference stage: tell them it's coming (as early as the screen) so it's never a surprise.
  • Rejections with grace — brief, kind, prompt. Those are the people whose experience matters most for our reputation.

30 · Playbooks

How I run each stage

Purpose, how to run it, what to listen for. This is technique; the question lists are in 90 and 91.

My style: guardrail question banks

  • Keep a bank of standard questions per job.
  • Let the conversation wander naturally; steer to any question when the moment opens.
  • Across all jobs, cover each question at least once — not every question on every job.

Three master tactics

  • Interrupt gracefully. You have to interrupt — you have to. Match their energy, reflect it ("wow, that sounds rough!"), then redirect: "You were telling me about that launch — how did it go?"
  • Ask "How?" relentlessly. "Great result — how did you get it?" is where a story holds together or unravels. Keep asking until you know whether it was them or the room.
  • Stop at the stop signs. Hesitation, a shift in tone, an answer that doesn't line up — slow down and drill in.

Stage 1 — Screen (15–30 min)

Purpose: rapport + basic fit. Worth another two hours? Treat it like a first date.

  • Set expectations: "First ~20 minutes I'll get to know you, then I'll answer your questions."
  • Let the candidate talk ~80% of the time. Listen for scope, what's driving the search, where they're dissatisfied.
  • Focus on the current role (and the last one if tenure is short). Not a full history.
  • Plant the TORC seed: "If we advance, we'll set up references — what will they say you're not good at?"
  • If it isn't landing, collapse the call and wrap in 15.

Listen for: goals that match the role; strengths with real examples; questions that show curiosity about the challenge (leading with hours/vacation is a yellow flag).

Stage 2 — Résumé Deep Dive (60 min) — the core

Purpose: good worker, self-motivated, results-driven — and the driver? Plus: did they understand X, not just do it?

  • Open with intros, refresh the role, let them ask questions (5–10 min).
  • Walk roles oldest → newest, ~10–15 min each, same guardrail questions per role — so I see the trajectory (up, flat, down).
  • Per job: charter → inherited → context → results → How? → biggest failure → manager → why they left.
  • Dig on "how" and impact. Separate solo heroes from lucky bystanders; run the Three P's.

Listen for: outcomes tied to expectations (A) vs. vague talk about events and people (B/C); push vs. pull on each exit; humility on failures; real understanding, not recall.

Stage 3 — Team Fit + Project Presentation (60 min)

Purpose: culture/DNA fit, and seeing them light up about real work.

  • The team meets the candidate; free-form, two-way Q&A.
  • They present a project (flagged in the email). Watch passion, depth, and how they handle questions.
  • Put teammates in front of them who represent where we want the culture to go.
  • Probe DNA: "tell me about a time you exhibited [trait]" (two or three examples), plus favorite/least-favorite job and best/worst boss.

Listen for: does the team come away energized or indifferent; does their ideal environment match ours; real curiosity in their questions.

After the loop

  • Debrief against the scorecard — role fit + company fit, scored — not who was most likable in the room.
  • Maybe = no. Reference every finalist before an offer.

40 · Pipeline

Sourcing to onboarding

The whole journey and the connective steps — sourcing, references, offer, onboarding.

  • 0. Scorecard first. No sourcing until it exists. Trim requirements, de-bias the posting, pick the blind step.
  • 1. Sourcing. Referrals first — "who are the most talented people you know?" — on a standing 30-min/week block. Plus recruiter, community sourcing, and a real job invitation (not a dry description).
  • 2. Application review. Did they meet requirements, answer the questions, show stability? Maybe = no; use paste-ready reasons.
  • 3. Screen (Stage 1). Pre-interview email, then 15–30 min; plant the TORC seed.
  • 4. Deep Dive (Stage 2). 60 min; job by job; was-it-really-them + light technical depth.
  • 5. Team Fit (Stage 3). 60 min; culture/DNA + project presentation.

Runs through every step: set the stage. Email before each interview, reiterate the agenda in the first few minutes, communicate promptly at every hand-off — so everyone leaves respecting us, hired or not.

6. References — "trust but verify"

  • Every finalist. Rockstars welcome it; B/C players stall.
  • Front-door + backdoor (people I find on LinkedIn). Mix of bosses, peers, subordinates — and I pick some myself.
  • Phone only (~15–20 min, confidential). People won't put negatives in writing.
  • Listen for enthusiasm. "Faint praise is damning praise" — hedging is the real signal.

7. Offer

  • Sell across the five F's — fit / family / freedom / fortune / fun — and keep selling through acceptance.
  • Comp against my bands, not their salary history; protect internal equity.
  • Deliver the "advocate for you" two-way accountability script.

8. Onboarding

  • Spell out first week / month / quarter priorities.
  • Cover communication preferences, the business, the team, the product, their aspirations.
  • Early, mine their network for the next referrals.
  • Make a deliberate stay/go read in the first ~30 days (skill / will / feedback).

90 · Question bank

Copy-paste ready

The reusable questions, grouped by stage — the doc I keep open in the room.

Stage 1 — Screen

  • Give me 60 seconds on what you're working on these days.
  • Why are you entertaining opportunities right now?
  • What are you looking for in your next role? What matters as you look at companies?
  • What attracted you to this role / company?
  • What are your career goals and aspirations?
  • (TORC) If we advance, we'll set up references — what will they say you're not good at?
  • [If needed] What are your salary expectations?

Stage 2 — Deep Dive

The full running order — Scratchpad → Roles → Scenarios → Closing — lives in section 91 below. Kept separate so it's the one doc I have open during the interview.

Stage 3 — Team Fit + Project

  • (Project) Walk us through a project you're proud of — what it was, why it mattered, your role, the hard parts.
  • Tell me about a time you exhibited [DNA trait]. (Then two or three more examples.)
  • Favorite job ever / least favorite job ever — why?
  • Best boss ever / worst manager ever — why?
  • The work environment you thrived in most / found most difficult — why?

References — phone, 15–20 min, confidential

  • In what context did you work with them?
  • What were their biggest strengths — areas where they were in the top 10%?
  • What were their biggest areas for improvement back then?
  • (Managers) How were they as a leader? What was their style, and how did staff respond?
  • On a 1–10 scale, how would you rate their overall performance? What drives that rating?
  • They mentioned they struggled with ___ — can you tell me more?
  • What's the one thing they could have done to be more effective?
  • What led to their departure?
  • What can we do to make them successful here?
  • How do they deal with conflict in the workplace?

Listen for: enthusiasm. Hesitation and faint praise are the real signal — a genuine A-Player reference brims with obvious admiration.

Reject-reason notepad

  • Does not meet requirements for the job
  • Did not answer the questions
  • Job hopping
  • Not the right fit

91 · Deep-dive role bank

The running order

Stage 2 exactly as I run it: Scratchpad → Roles → Scenarios → Closing. The Roles block is universal; Scenarios is the only role-specific part.

How I use this in the room

  • The Roles questions are guardrails: walk roles oldest → newest and steer to them as the conversation opens.
  • Won't hit every question on every job — cover each at least once across all jobs.
  • Ask "How?" relentlessly; interrupt gracefully; drill at any inconsistency.

1 · Scratchpad (opener)

  • Once onboarded and understanding the context here, what are the first places you'll want to explore from a [role] perspective?
  • (Show our process flow — e.g., the Miro board.)

2 · Roles (per-job bank — ask across each job, oldest → newest)

Ready to use as-is for any role. The starting point when you have nothing.

  • Why did you choose to join the company?
  • What did you inherit coming into this role?
  • What was the organizational context? Peers, manager, big team or small?
  • What were your quantifiable accomplishments in this role?
  • How did you achieve those results?
  • What was your biggest failure in the role?
  • What challenges did you face and how did you overcome them?
  • What was your manager's style and how did you work together?
  • What did you enjoy most and least about the role?
  • If we get to references, what should I expect to hear from your manager?
  • Why did you choose to move on from this role?

Probes to layer under the above

  • (Charter) What were you hired to do — how was success measured?
  • How much were you the driver vs. the team?
  • How were you measured?
  • Three P's: vs. last year? vs. the plan? vs. your peers?
  • (Light technical depth) Not just "did you do X" — "did you understand X?"

3 · Scenarios (the only part you rebuild per role)

Build-your-own template · brand-new role

  • Core convictions — "what strong convictions do you hold about [domain]?"
  • Scenario / judgment — a real situation from our world: "here's our process today; where does [role] fit and what value would that bring?"
  • Technical judgment — "when is X worth doing / how do you know it's working?"
  • Working style — process improvements that worked / didn't; an impossible-deadline story.
  • Passion signal — "if you gave a conference talk, what would it be about?"

Listen for: depth and taste on convictions; sound reasoning on the scenario (rarely one right answer); genuine energy on the passion question.

QA worked example

ConvictionsWhat strong convictions do you have about quality and QA testing? · How do you gauge your contribution — what makes you feel valuable? · Describe your ideal QA role.

Scenario 1 — SDLC fitHere's our process flow and where QA sits today (walk the Miro board) — where do you see QA fitting, and what value would that bring? · How would QA fit an anytime-deployment paradigm? · Testing in production? · Closing loops: how do you find where bugs come from?

Scenario 2 — automation & test judgmentWhen is it worth writing an automated test, and how do you know you're getting value? · What about a flaky/intermittent test — and the dangers of flaky automation? · We have 78 e2e smoke tests that take 12 minutes to run — thoughts? · Given a ticket with acceptance criteria, how would you test it?

Working style & passionWhat process improvements worked — and which didn't? · Ever had an impossible deadline? · If you gave a conference talk, what would it be about?

4 · Closing questions

  • What are you looking for — the environment you want, how you want to be empowered, what decisions you want to make?
  • Where are you in your job search? Other offers? If you had multiple, what 3 criteria decide which one you take?
