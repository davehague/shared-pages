Trust, but transcribe: what happened when I tried to teach my tutor app Italian

I've been building myself AI tutor apps for a while now, and the framework works: an audio lecture I can listen to on a walk, a Socratic lesson where the tutor makes me prove I actually understand, office hours for the questions the lecture raised, and flashcards that harvest themselves from what I got right. I've used it to learn toxicology for work and ML theory for fun, and adding a new subject is mostly a matter of pointing the engine at new source material.

So when my wife wanted to learn Italian, I figured Italian was just the next subject. Drop it in, generate the lectures, done by the weekend. It was not the next subject. Language learning bent that framework until it broke, and the interesting part is where it broke, because every break taught me something about building non-English features that I haven't seen written down anywhere. Here's what I tried, where it fell apart, and what worked in the end.

Road bump one: a language is not a subject

The first version went into the existing engine like any other topic, and the engine did its job: it produced an immersion-style Italian path, in Italian. Which is great if you already read Italian, and useless if you're starting from zero. What a beginner needs is closer to those classic guided audio courses: an English instructor who teaches and prompts you, and a native voice that lands the actual Italian, back and forth, with pauses for you to attempt an answer out loud.

That's a different learning model, and it also surfaced a problem no other subject has: my English instructor voice cannot pronounce Italian. Ask an English TTS voice to say "abitare" and you get something no Italian would recognize. So the lessons became two voices spliced together, segment by segment (English instructor, then native Italian voice, then back), with a hard rule enforced in code that Italian text never rides in the English voice. Language learning ended up as its own app with its own rules, sharing the philosophy but not the engine. The framework didn't survive contact, and that's fine. Frameworks are supposed to break where the domain is genuinely different.

Road bump two: the audio API lied to me

Then single Italian words started disappearing. A lesson would say "the word for to eat is..." and there'd be a gap or a chopped fragment where "mangiare" should be. Full sentences were fine, lone words died, and the API (Deepgram's Aura-2 Italian voices, as of late July 2026) returned a clean HTTP 200 every time. It was synthesizing silence for one-word requests about a third of the time and calling it success.

My first fix made it worse, and it's the failure I most want to share. The plan was to request raw audio, measure loudness, and retry silent renders. Sensible, except the validation measured peak volume across the whole API response, and the response was a WAV file, which starts with a text header (the letters RIFF and WAVE, sizes, format info). Read those header bytes as audio samples and they look like a full-volume signal, so every silent render sailed through the check. I was grading the envelope instead of the letter.

Road bump three: the audio that passed every check was still wrong

Once the validation measured actual speech, the deeper problem surfaced: renders that were loud enough and long enough were mispronounced. I asked for "abitare" and got back audio that transcribes as "avvitare" (to screw) or "auditare." A lesson that was already live had taught the word for "to understand" as "cacchiere," which isn't Italian at all.

I don't speak Italian and my wife is just starting, so neither of us could catch this by ear. Every safeguard a programmer would reach for (status codes, retries, duration checks, volume checks) waved confident nonsense straight through. The check that finally worked was cheap: transcribe your own audio and diff it against what you asked for. I ran suspect renders through two separate speech-to-text engines (a local Whisper model plus Deepgram's own nova-2, which meant their STT was convicting their TTS), on the theory that one model's blind spot shouldn't get to hide the other's. Trust, but transcribe.

What worked: boring voices and a second opinion

The root cause was the class of voice. Generative TTS synthesizes fresh audio every request, so the same input can come out different (or wrong) on any call, and I tested all nine of Aura-2's Italian voices without finding one that was reliably safe. Older-style deterministic voices (I landed on Google's Neural2) return byte-identical audio for identical input, every time. They sound a little less human, and I'll take that trade without blinking, because a voice that cannot improvise cannot teach my wife a word that doesn't exist. Where correctness outranks expressiveness, boring-reliable is the feature.

The lesson text needed the same treatment one layer up. My generator model (GLM 5.2) sometimes writes English-flavored Italian ("chiedere una domanda," a word-for-word calque of "ask a question" that Italians don't say), so every generated script now passes through a second model acting as reviewer before it gets voiced. Picking that reviewer was an afternoon and under a dollar: I planted known traps (false friends, calques, wrong-verb swaps) alongside clean control sentences and ran nine models over the set, then a harder second round for the ones that aced the first. Gemini 3.6 Flash won it clean, every planted error caught with zero false alarms. GLM missed exactly the error class it produces itself, so don't let a model grade its own homework. And Mistral Large confidently "corrected" a sentence that was already right (it insisted "sposato con Anna" should be "sposato a Anna," which is wrong), which is worse than missing an error, because a reviewer that invents fixes injects the bugs it exists to catch.

If your app speaks a language you don't

  • Expect your framework to break. A new language is a new domain, not a new subject.
  • Give one voice one language. Splice segments; never make the English voice attempt the target language.
  • Transcribe your own audio output and diff it against what you asked for, with two transcribers from different vendors.
  • Prefer deterministic voices when correctness outranks expressiveness.
  • Validate the content, not the container. Status codes, file sizes, and volume levels all lied to me in one week.
  • Review generated text with a different model than the one that wrote it, and test the reviewer with planted errors before trusting it.

The version that works today has the guided two-voice lessons, office hours, and self-harvesting flashcards, in Italian, with pronunciation I've verified by machine instead of by ear. Every check in that list is a few dozen lines an agent can write in minutes. I still can't speak Italian, and I can ship Italian I trust, because the pipeline has ears now. What's your app saying that you can't hear? Go find out.
